Citing a Source: Starter Card

A citation is a return ticket: it lets you (or anyone) walk back to the exact record you saw. You do not need a 60-page style manual to start. You need five facts, in this order.

The five-fact citation card

Copy this fill-in-the-blank card onto an index card or into your notes app:

WHO made the record:      [agency / church / newspaper / person]
WHAT kind of record:      [birth certificate / 1910 census / obituary]
WHEN it was created:      [date the record was made]
WHERE you saw it:         [website + page or archive + box]
WHEN you looked:          [date viewed]

Then write the actual person line first, like this:

Mary Ellen Doyle (1851-1924); death certificate #8842, Cook County, Illinois,
issued 4 Feb 1924; Illinois State Archives, Springfield; viewed 27 Oct 2026
at FamilySearch "Illinois, Cook County, vital records," image 11 of 40.

Why these five facts

FactWithout it, what breaks
Record-creatorTwo "birth records" in the same town tell different stories
Record typeA "draft card" and a "pension file" about the same man disagree
Creation dateLater copies get made with errors; you must say which one you read
Where seenImages move between sites; the archive box never moves
Date viewedSites change. "Viewed 27 Oct 2026" makes your claim checkable forever

Three shortcuts most beginners miss

  1. Bracket your uncertainty. If you inferred something ("probably the same

Thomas Murphy because the address matches"), write it in square brackets: [same Thomas Murphy? 88 N. Clark St. matches both records]. Inference plus honesty beats confident error.

  1. Cite the image, not the transcription. FamilySearch's index says one

thing, the handwritten record says another. Always point to the image you actually read, and add "image 11 of 40" so you can find it again.

  1. Reuse, do not retype. You will cite the same source many times. Save one

"source card" per source (a note or a page in your binder) and reference it: "Source 12" - defined once on the Source 12 card.

The one-sentence rule

If a stranger with a library card and patience could find exactly what you saw with your citation and nothing else, it is a good citation. If they could not, add the missing fact.
